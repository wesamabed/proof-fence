# ProofFence's audit of itself

ProofFence v0.2 passed every mechanical check it defines for itself: `selftest`
proved for all 13 cases that the starter fails semantically and the reference
passes, and `mutation` reported **78 mutants killed, 0 survivors**.

It was then audited blind, against its own published text rather than against its
own graders. The audit found **20 of 138 graded fixtures underspecified**, in
**9 of the 13 cases**.

This document publishes that result. The defects are named, the repairs are
named, and the repairs that make a previously-failing answer *correct* are
identified as such. Nothing was quietly dropped.

---

## Headline numbers

| | v0.2 |
|---|---|
| Graded fixtures | **138** |
| Oracle-clean | **118** |
| Oracle-dirty | **20** |
| Cases with at least one dirty fixture | **9 of 13** |
| Cases fully clean | **4** — PF-001, PF-005, PF-007, PF-009 |
| Mechanical result on the same artifact | selftest 13/13, mutation 78 killed / 0 survivors |

Scoring in v0.2 was all-or-nothing per case, so one contested fixture flips an
entire case. Under that rule only **4 of 13 cases** were citable, and of those
four, two (PF-001, PF-009) state their whole rule in the task and are solvable by
transcription. **The only cases that were both oracle-clean and genuinely testing
the headline construct were PF-005 and PF-007.**

An "oracle-dirty" fixture is not a fixture with a wrong answer. It is a fixture
where a competent reader working only from the published task could reach a
different answer than the grader requires, because the rule the grader enforces
is not derivable from the text the candidate receives. That is a defect in the
benchmark, not in the candidate.

---

## How the audit was run

- **Two independent audits.** Two separately instantiated AI audit sessions
  received an identical brief and worked without communicating. They are not
  human raters and this document does not claim they are.
- **Blind to all candidate artifacts.** Every auditing session was barred by
  absolute path from any solution or result directory, so no session could see
  how any implementation had actually scored. All sessions recorded no barrier
  incidents.
- **Disputes resolved by measurement.** A third blind session adjudicated
  disagreements by writing the **competing implementations in Go** and compiling
  them against the unmodified trusted graders, recording exactly which fixtures
  diverge. The dirty-fixture counts above are measured, not argued.
- **Inter-rater disagreement is reported, not hidden.** The two auditors agreed
  exactly on 5 cases, substantively on 1, and disputed 7 — while independently
  reaching nearly the same aggregate by different per-case routes. Disagreement
  between two competent blind readers is itself direct evidence that a task is
  underspecified, which is why it is published rather than averaged away.
- **The audit was frozen and hashed before anything else happened.** It was
  written and sealed before any examination of how any implementation had scored,
  so no finding could have been chosen to explain an observed result.

One deliberate asymmetry is worth stating: the audit was commissioned by the
benchmark's own author, and the sessions that performed it are of the same
general kind as the systems the benchmark is designed to measure. That is a real
limitation on its independence. It is offered as a reproducible procedure others
can re-run — the graders, references and tasks are all in this repository — not
as third-party certification.

## Where the defects were

| Case | Dirty | Nature of the defect |
|---|---|---|
| PF-001 | 0 | — |
| PF-002 | 1 | "admitted" undefined; rejecting a member outside the vocabulary was a second, unstated rule |
| PF-003 | 1 | "object" appeared only in the accept clause and was never restated as a rejection rule |
| PF-004 | 1 | a source the task twice calls weak is silently promoted to a *necessary* conjunct |
| PF-005 | 0 | — |
| PF-006 | 1 | a stated fact (an unconfigured gate yields an empty string) with no stated consequence |
| PF-007 | 0 | — |
| PF-008 | 1 | same insufficient-implies-necessary defect as PF-004 |
| PF-009 | 0 | — |
| PF-010 | 1 | the task says the two observer lists can disagree without saying which governs |
| PF-011 | **4** | the RETAIN/QUARANTINE partition is author policy the text argues against |
| PF-012 | 1 | **reference overreach**: a guard stated nowhere and contradicted by the published rule |
| PF-013 | **9** | a corroboration requirement that exists only in a non-materialized comment |

### The three systemic findings

**1. The decision vocabulary never reached the solver.** The four-valued
vocabulary arrived only through four const doc comments in the starter.
`Materialize` copied `starter/` and `task.md` and nothing else; no task
referenced any file under `docs/`; and `case.json` — whose `invariant` field
frequently states a case's rule outright — was never materialized. So the
decisive gloss in `AUTHORITY_DECISION_MODEL.md`, that `RETAIN` is an affirmative
conclusion and `QUARANTINE` is the absence of one, never arrived — while each
task described `RETAIN` by a non-committal operational effect such as "re-queue
for another pass", pulling the opposite way. This single gap is upstream of most
of PF-011's and PF-013's contested fixtures.

**2. A published fixture-distribution table was wrong.** `CONSTRUCT_VALIDITY.md`
recorded PF-013 as GRANT 1 / RETAIN 2 / REVOKE 1 / QUARANTINE 15 against a stated
total of 24 — a row that sums to 19. The true v0.2 distribution was
**1 / 2 / 1 / 20**, meaning an unconditional `QUARANTINE` was correct on 20 of 24
fixtures, even though the committed `always-quarantine` mutant still died.

**3. The mutation score measured something other than what it was cited for.**
This is the audit's most consequential finding and it generalises well beyond
this repository:

> A mutation suite's pass criterion is `survivors = 0`, so **every committed
> mutant is by construction something the grader must kill**. When a defensible
> alternative reading of a task is committed as a mutant, the suite records its
> death as evidence of grader quality. Mutation testing applied to a benchmark
> grader does not merely fail to detect oracle ambiguity — **it converts oracle
> ambiguity into a reported discrimination success.**

Measured here, not argued: **four of the 78 committed mutants were exactly the
defensible alternative that produced their case's contested fixture** — PF-004
`ignores-payload`, PF-006 `unconfigured-gate-accepted`, PF-010
`observed-pass-only`, PF-011 `residue-quarantined`. The suite reported all four
as killed and scored 78/78.

**`78/78 killed` means this grader set is sensitive to this mutant set. It does
not mean the graders are correct.** Oracle validity requires independent
specification audit. v0.3 prints that caveat on every mutation run and requires
every committed mutant to carry a recorded kind.

## What was repaired in v0.3, and on what authority

Every repair below derives from the frozen audit, from semantics already public
in v0.2, or from general benchmark-design principles. **No repair was chosen
because of how any implementation had scored**, and the repairs were not tuned
against any observed output.

One governing principle covers most of them: **a rule a grader enforces must be
reachable by a solver.** In several cases the decisive semantics already existed
in the repository but lived in a file the harness never materializes — the
`case.json` `invariant` field, a `docs/` page, or a reference implementation's
doc comment. The repair is to materialize what was already written, not to invent
a new rule.

| Case | Fixtures | Disposition | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| all four-valued | — | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the generic vocabulary is appended to every four-valued `TASK.md` |
| PF-002 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the task states that a member outside the vocabulary is rejected, not dropped |
| PF-003 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the task states that a non-object JSON value is rejected, and flags the Go trap |
| PF-004 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the task states the serializer's rendering contract, making the inconsistency derivable |
| PF-006 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the task anchors the empty string outside the scope vocabulary |
| PF-008 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | as PF-004 |
| PF-010 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the task states the event-protocol fact from which the conjunction follows |
| PF-011 | 4 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | `Retrieved=false` now says the other fields are unpopulated; cursor semantics and the bounded-page rule are stated |
| PF-012 | 1 | **`CHANGE_ORACLE`** | the unstated guard is **deleted from the reference** |
| PF-013 | 8 | **`CHANGE_ORACLE`** | an attributable, definite readback decides; the service need not corroborate |
| PF-013 | 1 | `CLARIFY_SEMANTICS` | the "value outside this set" clause is extended to `Intent` |

### PF-012: a repair that makes a previously-failing answer correct

The v0.2 reference quarantined any request whose `Subject` was the empty string.
That guard is stated nowhere in the task, and the task's only statement about
subjects argues against it: *"Identifiers are opaque and compared literally"* —
under which `""` matches `""`. The sibling guard on `Generation` **is** publicly
grounded (*"Generation numbers start at 1"*), and PF-006 explicitly names its own
analogous degenerate input, so the author's own habits show the subject guard was
an oversight rather than a design choice.

**The guard was deleted from the reference.** The fixture now asserts the
published rule — a request and an observation naming the same empty identifier
name the same subject — and a symmetric fixture was added for an empty request
subject against a named observation subject.

This is recorded explicitly because it matters for the audit's credibility: **the
correct repair makes an answer that the v0.2 oracle marked wrong into a correct
answer.** A repair process that could only ever increase the apparent difficulty
of a benchmark would not be a repair process. Scientific validity takes
precedence over preserving an apparent failure.

### PF-013: restoring the construct rather than documenting the gap

PF-013's v0.2 grader required the key service's own report to be informative and
to agree with the independent readback before any final decision. That rule
existed only in the reference's doc comment. The task instead builds the readback
up as the authoritative modality — *"a separate reader, holding its own
credential and talking to the storage layer rather than to the key service, reads
the slot directly and signs what it read"* — and supplies preconditions (INSTALL
only from an empty slot, REMOVE only from an occupied one) that make an
attributable readback self-sufficient.

The sharpest instance: REMOVE, the service could not report what happened, and an
attributable direct read shows the slot **empty**. `REVOKE` is verifiably correct;
the v0.2 grader demanded `QUARANTINE`. That inverts the benchmark's own stated
position — `AUTHORITY_DECISION_MODEL.md` says answering `QUARANTINE` when the
evidence is determinate is an error, and `PILOT_PROTOCOL.md` requires
over-refusal to be reported as a distinct error class. A grader that *penalises a
solver for declining to over-refuse* is measuring the opposite of what the case
claims.

Two repairs were available. Stating the corroboration rule in the task would have
made the oracle learnable, but would have changed PF-013 from a measure of
whether a solver stops short of an unsupported conclusion into a measure of
whether it applies a stated conjunction — a real loss. **Changing the oracle was
chosen instead**, because it makes the case measure what it claims. An
attributable, definite readback now decides; the service's report remains
decisive only where it *contradicts* the readback, which is publicly grounded in
`QUARANTINE`'s own definition ("or material evidence conflicts").

A side effect worth naming: PF-013's fixture distribution moved from 1/2/1/20 to
4/4/4/12. An unconditional `QUARANTINE` was correct on 83% of the case's fixtures
before the repair and is correct on 50% after it.

## What the audit does not say

It does not say the benchmark is bad. 118 of 138 fixtures were sound. PF-005
grades all eight states of its input space; PF-008 publicly discloses its Go
zero-value trap without telegraphing the answer; PF-011's cursor semantics
correctly separate "known to contain residue" from "not proven empty"; and the
contamination discipline throughout is exemplary and does not overclaim. The
defects were concentrated precisely where the headline construct lives, which is
the least convenient place for them to be and the reason they are published.

It also says nothing whatsoever about any model, and this document names none.
The audit examined a benchmark against its own text. It did not evaluate any
system's capability, and no capability claim anywhere in this repository rests on
it. See `README.md` for ProofFence's evaluation status.

## The v0.3 re-audit, which found three more

Repairing a benchmark on the strength of one audit and then calling it clean would
repeat the mistake this document is about. So the repaired v0.3 candidate was
audited again, from scratch, by **two fresh sessions that had not seen the audit
above** and were barred from it until they had frozen their own per-fixture
judgments.

They were given exactly what a solver receives — the materialized `TASK.md` and
starter for each case — plus the graders they were auditing. Both used the same
method as before: derive the answer from the public text first, then open the
grader, then **write the competing implementation and compile it against the
unmodified grader** to measure the exact divergence set.

**Auditor A: 139 of 139 clean.** **Auditor B: 136 of 139 clean, 3 dirty.**

B was right, and the three fixtures were real.

### What B found

`TestMissingReadbackRecord`, `TestUnattributableReadback` and
`TestUnattributableReadbackOnRemoval` in PF-013 all require `QUARANTINE` purely
because no attributable readback exists, while the key service's own report is
definite and uncontradicted. The rule being enforced — *an attributable readback
is necessary for any conclusion* — appeared nowhere in the task. It lived only in
the reference implementation's doc comment.

B's structural argument was decisive: **every other case in the benchmark that
needs a source to be insufficient says so in one explicit sentence.** PF-004 and
PF-008 say the payload "reports what the service accepted rather than what the
storage layer now holds". PF-007 says any caller "can put either value in that
field". PF-010 says the summary is written "by the job's own code". PF-011 says
closing the ticket "asserts both that the purge ran to completion and that nothing
was left behind". PF-013 alone had no such sentence.

Measured, not argued: an implementation that falls back to a definite service
report when no usable readback exists passes 21 of 24 and fails exactly those three.

This is the **same defect class** the v0.2 audit named — *non-sufficiency does not
entail necessity* — approached from the opposite side. The v0.2 repair established
that an attributable readback is **sufficient**. Neither the v0.2 audit nor the
v0.3 repair had examined whether it is **necessary**. The gap survived both.

### The repair, and its verification

One paragraph was added to PF-013's task, matching PF-010's existing convention:

> "The service is reporting on its own action, from its own records; it does not
> read the slot. Read together with the preconditions above, a report that the
> service carried out or declined the request amounts to a claim about what the
> slot now holds. But an account a system gives of its own action does not
> establish what is actually stored there."

The middle clause is deliberate: four conflict fixtures need the report to still
carry a **claim** about the slot, so that the readback can contradict it. Removing
the claim entirely would have broken them, and a probe that treats the report as
inert diverges on exactly those four.

The first draft of that clause read "says what the slot **should** now hold", and
an auditor caught a real residual in it: a should-claim and an is-finding do not
strictly contradict, so two of the four conflict fixtures still carried a second
defensible answer. Changing it to "amounts to a claim about what the slot now
holds" — a present-tense assertion about the same fact the readback reports —
closed it.

**Both auditors independently re-verified the final shipped text** and measured
three probes with identical results: the disputed reading diverges on exactly 3,
the report-inert reading on exactly 4, the should-versus-is reading on exactly 2.
**PF-013: 0 of 24 dirty. Suite: 0 of 139.**

One methodological point from that round is worth keeping, because it is the
distinction the whole procedure rests on. A divergence count **cannot fall** in
response to a prose change — the grader never reads the task. Rewording does not
shrink the blast radius; it removes the *justification*, which is what converts a
divergence from a **benchmark defect** into a **solver error**. Measurement fixes
how many fixtures are at stake. Only reading the published text decides whose
fault they are.

### What the re-audit changes about the numbers

| | v0.2 | v0.3 |
|---|---|---|
| Graded fixtures | 138 | **139** |
| Oracle-dirty | **20** | **0** |
| Cases with at least one dirty fixture | 9 of 13 | **0 of 13** |
| Cases citable under all-or-nothing scoring | 4 of 13 | **13 of 13** |

### Residual risk, disclosed rather than smoothed over

Zero dirty is not zero doubt, and both auditors named where their confidence is
lower. The thinnest **clean** inferences in the suite are:

- **PF-004 / PF-008, `SUCCEEDED` alongside a non-empty `FaultCode`** (3 fixtures).
  Both auditors independently rated these clean at **MEDIUM** confidence: the
  defeating force of a recorded fault is argued from the recorder's stated
  independence plus the task's "establishes that the record is gone" standard,
  rather than stated outright. They were **deliberately not repaired**, because the
  audit did not call for it and changing the instrument after the audit would make
  the published count a moving target. The inference is instead recorded in each
  case's `limitations`.
- **PF-011's four inconclusive-listing fixtures.** The chain is complete and every
  link is public, but it spans four separate sentences against a case gloss
  ("re-queue the workspace for another decommissioning pass") that pulls the other
  way. Both auditors rated them clean; A named this the largest residual ambiguity
  in the suite.
- **A cross-case asymmetry worth knowing about:** an unmodelled enum value yields
  `QUARANTINE` in PF-011 and PF-012 but `GRANT`/`REVOKE` in PF-013. One rule
  reconciles them — an unmodelled value contributes no information, and the
  decision then follows from whether the *remaining* evidence establishes a
  conclusion — and each case's text reaches it. But a solver who generalises
  "unmodelled enum ⇒ QUARANTINE" across cases loses exactly two fixtures.

### What the re-audit says about mutation scores, again

Between them the two auditors wrote **24 competing implementations** and compiled
them against the unmodified graders. **Every one of them would have been recorded
as a killed mutant.** Only one exposed a real oracle defect. A kill count cannot
tell the two apart; only reading the published text can.

## Reproducing it

The material the audit used is all here: `cases/*/task.md` is what a solver
receives (plus the submission boundary and, for four-valued cases, the decision
vocabulary), `cases/*/grader/` is the oracle, and `cases/*/reference/` is the
implementation the oracle was built around. The audit's method was:

1. read only the task, and derive the answer for each fixture;
2. compare with the grader's requirement;
3. where they differ, write the competing implementation and compile it against
   the unmodified grader to measure exactly which fixtures diverge;
4. classify the case, and record the decisive quotation from the public text.

Step 3 is what makes a dirty-fixture count a measurement rather than an opinion,
and it is the step most worth copying. Anyone who re-runs this on v0.3 and finds
a fixture we missed is invited to say so — that is the point of publishing it.
